Hypothesis tests

Now turn estimation into a decision rule

Hypothesis testing
assume a reference world → ask how unusual our statistic is

Scientific hypothesis ≠ statistical hypothesis

Do not confuse the levels
Scientific questionDoes the treatment improve cardiovascular health?
↓ operationalize
Clinical endpointsystolic blood pressure after treatment
↓ formalize
Statistical hypothesis\(H_0:\Delta=0\) versus an alternative

The statistical hypothesis is narrower than the scientific claim. Rejecting a BP null hypothesis does not prove the full clinical hypothesis.

Step 1: define the null reference

A specific parameter statement
Null\(H_0:\Delta=0\)no population mean difference in this example
Alternative\(H_1:\Delta\ne0\)population means differ
Not always “no difference”

Equivalence and non-inferiority questions use different null hypotheses. We meet them next week.

What changes when \(n\) changes?

Precision is not magnitude
\(n\uparrow\)\(\mathrm{SE}\downarrow\)sampling distribution narrows
\(n\uparrow\)CI narrowsestimate becomes more precise

This is why “statistically significant” cannot substitute for “important.”

Effect size asks “how much?”

Magnitude on different scales
RAW EFFECT \(\hat\Delta = \bar{x}_T - \bar{x}_C\) mmHg · often easiest to interpret clinically
STANDARDIZED EFFECT \(d = \frac{\bar{x}_T - \bar{x}_C}{s_{\mathrm{pooled}}}\) difference expressed in SD units
Standardized ≠ automatically meaningful

Clinical relevance still needs a domain-specific threshold and benefit–risk context.

Which error is worse?

There is no universal answer

Two important concepts

Power depends on a specified alternative.
Sample-size planning therefore needs an effect size worth detecting—not just an arbitrary desire for “80% power.”
Non-significant does not mean equivalent.
\(p\ge\alpha\) means

Misconception check

Decide before revealing
“\(p=0.03\) means there is a 97% chance the treatment works.”
False · p is calculated assuming \(H_0\).
“A 95% CI gives the plausible effect magnitude, not just a yes/no answer.”
Yes · that is one reason intervals are so useful.
“If \(p=0.20\), we proved there is no effect.”
False · failure to reject is not proof of equivalence.
“With a huge \(n\), a tiny effect may become statistically significant.”
Yes · precision increases with sample size.

Would you trust this conclusion?
