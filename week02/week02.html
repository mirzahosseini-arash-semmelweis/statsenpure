Two fundamentally different worlds

Discrete versus continuous
Discrete random variable
Possible values are separated. Individual values can have positive probability.
Fair die: \(X\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\)
Continuous random variable
Possible values fill an interval. Probability belongs to ranges, not exact points.
Random number: \(X\in[0,1]\)

Discrete probability lives on values. Continuous probability lives over intervals.

Use a fair die rather than a coin as the flagship discrete-uniform example. Reserve the coin for Bernoulli.

The main conceptual target: for continuous variables, asking for exactly one value is not the same kind of question as for discrete variables.

Meet the normal distribution

After the intuition, the notation
Notation
\(X\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\)
Two parameters uniquely define the model: location and spread.
Density
\[f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac12\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}\]
Recognize the formula. Do not memorize it.

For the Gaussian model, \(\mu\) moves the center and \(\sigma\) controls the spread.

Why Gaussian normal distribution is so common in nature: it models an outcome that is the sum of many tiny independent contributions.

Tell students that formula recognition is enough here. They should understand what the parameters do and how to interpret probability regions.

Mean: where is the distribution centered?

Expected value as a balance point

\[ E[X] = \sum_x x\,p(x) \quad\text{or}\quad E[X]=\int x f(x)\,dx \]

\(E[X]\)

The expected value (mean, also called first moment) is the balancing point of the probability distribution.

Use this as a physical metaphor, then remind students that the mean can be pulled by long tails. That sets up skewness.

Variance: how far do values wander?

Second central moment

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2], \qquad SD(X)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)} \]

Variance (also called second central moment) measures spread around the mean using squared deviations.

Students should know variance is in squared units and standard deviation returns to original units. Use tablet mass: variance in g² is less intuitive than SD in g.

Discrete: one trial to many trials

Binary and finite equal-chance mechanisms
Degenerate
The outcome is fixed.
\(\mathbb{P}(X=c)=1\)
Discrete uniform
Finite outcomes equally likely.
\(\mathbb{P}(X=x)=1/k\)
Bernoulli
One yes/no experiment.
\(\mathbb{P}(X=1)=p\)
Binomial
Successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials.
\(\mathbb{P}(X=k)=\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}\)
Coin toss: Bernoulli. Fair die: discrete uniform. Repeated responses: binomial.

Do not spend time deriving each formula. The story/mechanism is the learning target. The binomial formula will be used in exercises.

Discrete: counting and sampling

Different sampling mechanisms create different distributions
Geometric
Trials until the first success.
\(\mathbb{P}(T=t)=(1-p)^{t-1}p\)
Hypergeometric
Sampling from a finite batch without replacement.
\(\mathbb{P}(X=k)=\frac{\binom Kk\binom{N-K}{n-k}}{\binom Nn}\)
Poisson
Count events in a fixed exposure interval.
\(\mathbb{P}(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\)

Before calculating, identify the experiment that generated the count.

Use the tablet press example for geometric, batch sampling for hypergeometric, and dispensing errors or adverse events per exposure interval for Poisson.

Pharmaceutical examples: concentration and clearance often right-skew; assay errors may be approximately Gaussian after calibration; adverse event counts may use Poisson-like models; extreme values require caution.

The catch

Week 2 synthesis
A distribution describes how probability is allocated.
Its shape determines which observations are plausible.
Different scientific mechanisms generate different distribution families.

But in real science, the true distribution and its parameters are almost never known.

Next week: how can incomplete observations teach us about the process that generated them?

End with the transition from probability to statistics. Probability assumes a model and asks what observations are likely. Statistics observes data and asks what model or parameter values are plausible.
